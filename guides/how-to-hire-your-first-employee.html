How to hire your first employee

Your first hire is the moment the business stops being just you. Here's how to know you're ready, stay compliant and choose well.

By the Business4U Editorial Team. Updated November 12, 2026. 10-minute read.

Signs you're ready

  • You're turning away work or delaying sales because you're at capacity.
  • You spend hours on tasks someone else could do well for less than your time is worth.
  • Revenue has been steady for several months and can cover the full cost of the role plus a buffer.

Check the true cost with the employee cost calculator, then add the payment to your runway forecast.

Employee or contractor?

Contractors control how they work, use their own tools, can work for others and invoice you. Employees work under your direction and on your schedule. Tax authorities look at the real relationship, not the label, and misclassifying an employee as a contractor can lead to back taxes and penalties. If you're unsure, check your tax authority's guidance or ask an accountant. Starting with a contractor for a defined project is a good way to test a role.

Register as an employer

CountryTypical steps
United StatesGet an EIN; register for state payroll tax and unemployment accounts; verify eligibility to work with Form I-9; collect Form W-4; report new hires to the state; arrange workers' compensation insurance as your state requires.
CanadaOpen a payroll program account with the CRA; collect a TD1 form; deduct CPP, EI and income tax; register with your provincial workers' compensation board where required; issue a Record of Employment when employment ends.
United KingdomRegister as an employer with HMRC and run payroll through PAYE; check the right to work; enrol eligible staff in a workplace pension; get employers' liability insurance.

This is a summary, not a complete list. Check your government's official employer guidance.

Write a job post people want to answer

  • Lead with the outcomes the role owns, not a list of adjectives.
  • State pay, hours, location or remote policy. Many places now require pay ranges in job ads.
  • List must-haves separately from nice-to-haves.
  • Describe what working with you is actually like.

Find candidates

Your network and customers often produce the best first hires. Add local job boards, industry groups, college career centres and general job sites. Ask for a short task-based answer in the application to filter quickly.

Interview with structure

  1. A 20-minute screening call on motivation, availability and pay expectations.
  2. A structured interview: ask every candidate the same questions about real past situations.
  3. A short, paid work sample that mirrors the job.
  4. Two reference checks focused on specific behaviours.

Make the offer and onboard well

Put the offer in writing: role, pay, hours, start date, probation, benefits and key policies. Plan the first week in detail: tools and logins ready, a written guide to how things work, a first small win, and a check-in at the end of week one and month one.

Frequently asked questions

Should my first hire be full-time?

Not necessarily. Part-time or a fixed-term contract lowers the risk while you learn what the role really needs.

Do I need an employee handbook?

A short one covering pay, hours, leave, conduct and safety prevents misunderstandings. Some places require certain written policies.

Should I use a payroll service?

For most small employers, yes. It reduces errors and missed filings.
