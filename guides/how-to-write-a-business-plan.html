How to write a business plan lenders actually read

A business plan is a sales document for your idea and a thinking tool for you. Here's what goes in each section, how long it should be, and what readers look for first.

By the Business4U Editorial Team. Updated November 12, 2026. 11-minute read.

Lean plan or traditional plan?

A lean plan fits on one page: customer, problem, solution, revenue model, channels, costs and milestones. It's ideal for testing and steering a new business. Build one in minutes with our one-page plan builder. A traditional plan, usually 15 to 30 pages, is what banks, government loan programs and some investors expect. Write the lean version first; the traditional one is an expansion of it.

The sections of a traditional plan

1. Executive summary

One page that could stand alone: what you sell, to whom, why you'll win, how much money you need, what it pays for, and the key numbers. Write it last. Many readers decide whether to keep reading here.

2. Company description

Legal structure, location, owners, mission and stage. Keep it factual.

3. Market analysis

Define your target customer precisely and estimate how many of them you can realistically reach. Bottom-up estimates beat top-down ones: "there are 4,200 dental practices within our delivery area, and we expect to win 2% in year one" is more credible than "the dental market is worth billions".

4. Competition

Name direct competitors and the alternatives customers use today, including doing nothing. A simple comparison table on price, quality, speed and convenience shows where you fit.

5. Products and services

What you sell, how it's delivered, and your pricing with the reasoning behind it. Link price to customer value and to your margins.

6. Marketing and sales

Which channels you'll use, what each will cost, and what conversion you expect. Show the funnel: how many people need to see you to produce one customer. Our 90-day marketing plan guide helps.

7. Operations

Location, equipment, suppliers, technology, key processes and any licences or permits. Lenders want to see you've thought through how the work actually gets done.

8. Management and team

Who does what and why they're qualified. Gaps are fine if you show how you'll fill them, with hires, advisors or contractors.

9. Financial plan

Usually three years of projections, with monthly detail for year one:

  • Profit and loss forecast: revenue, cost of sales, operating expenses, profit.
  • Cash flow forecast: when money actually comes in and goes out. This is the statement lenders scrutinise most.
  • Balance sheet: assets, liabilities and equity.
  • Break-even analysis: use the break-even calculator.
  • Assumptions: list every important assumption so readers can test it.

10. Funding request

How much, what type (loan, equity, grant), what it will be spent on, and how it will be repaid or what investors receive. Use the loan calculator to show repayments fit inside your cash flow.

11. Appendix

Resumes, letters of intent, quotes, permits, market research and detailed financial tables.

What lenders look for first

  • Can cash flow comfortably cover the loan payments?
  • How much of your own money is in the business?
  • Are the assumptions realistic and backed by evidence?
  • What's the plan if sales come in 30% below forecast?

Common mistakes

  • Hockey-stick revenue with no explanation of where customers come from.
  • Forgetting owner pay, taxes, insurance or loan repayments in costs.
  • Writing about the product for pages and the customer for a paragraph.
  • A plan that is never updated after it's written.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a business plan be?

One page for internal use; roughly 15 to 30 pages plus appendices for a bank or formal program. Shorter and clearer beats longer.

Do I need a business plan to get a loan?

Many lenders and most government-backed programs ask for one, especially for startups without trading history.

Should I pay someone to write it?

You should write it, because you need to understand and defend every number. Paying an advisor to review it can be worthwhile.
