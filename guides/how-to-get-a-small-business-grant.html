How to find and win a small business grant

Grants are real but narrower than the internet suggests. Here's where they come from, how to find the ones you actually qualify for, and how to write an application that stands out.

By the Business4U Editorial Team. Updated November 12, 2026. 9-minute read.

A reality check first

Grants are money you don't repay, which is exactly why they're competitive and targeted. Very few governments hand out general-purpose grants to start an ordinary business. Most grants fund something specific: research and development, exporting, hiring, energy efficiency, a particular region or industry, or founders from groups that are under-represented in business. Treat grants as a bonus layer on top of a business that can also be funded another way.

Where grants come from

  • National governments: innovation and R&D programs (for example SBIR and STTR in the US, NRC IRAP in Canada, Innovate UK), export support and sector programs.
  • State, provincial and city governments: local economic development grants, often tied to jobs, location or revitalisation.
  • Corporations: brand-sponsored grant programs and contests, often for small or community-focused businesses.
  • Foundations and nonprofits: programs for specific founder groups, social enterprises or regions.
  • Competitions: pitch contests and awards with cash prizes, including our own Founder Pitch of the Month.

How to find grants you qualify for

  1. Start with official finders: Grants.gov and your state's economic development agency in the US, Innovation Canada's Business Benefits Finder, business.gov.au, and your national innovation agency.
  2. Ask your local Small Business Development Center, Community Futures office or chamber of commerce. They know local programs that never rank in search results.
  3. Search for your industry or founder group plus "grant" and your region.
  4. Browse our funding finder and filter by country and "Grant".
  5. Set a calendar: many programs open once or twice a year.

Writing an application that wins

  • Read the scoring criteria first and mirror their language. Reviewers score against a rubric; make it easy for them to tick boxes.
  • Lead with outcomes, not needs. "This grant lets us hire two local staff and reach 400 new customers within 12 months" beats "we need money to grow".
  • Show a credible budget that matches the activities line by line, including any matching funds you'll contribute.
  • Prove you can deliver with traction, the team's experience, letters of support and a realistic timeline.
  • Make it measurable: jobs created, revenue, customers served, emissions cut.
  • Submit early. Portals crash on deadline day, and missing documents disqualify otherwise great applications.

A strong business plan makes every application faster, because most of the content is already written.

How to spot grant scams

Legitimate government grants don't charge a fee to apply or to "release" funds, and they don't contact you out of the blue to say you've won. Never pay for a "guaranteed grant", share bank login details, or send gift cards. Verify any program on its official government website before applying.

If you don't win

Ask for reviewer feedback; many programs provide it. Improve and reapply next round. Meanwhile, look at loans with favourable terms, such as microloans and government-backed programs, in the funding options guide.

Frequently asked questions

Are grants taxable?

Often, yes. Many grants count as business income. Ask your accountant how to report them.

Can a sole proprietor get a grant?

Some programs accept sole proprietors; others require incorporation. Check eligibility rules carefully.

Should I pay a grant writer?

For large, complex grants it can be worth it, but avoid anyone charging large upfront fees or promising success.
