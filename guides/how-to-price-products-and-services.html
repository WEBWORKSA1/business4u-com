How to price products and services

Price is the fastest lever on profit you have. Here's a method for setting prices that cover your costs, match what customers value and leave room to grow.

By the Business4U Editorial Team. Updated November 12, 2026. 10-minute read.

Why pricing matters more than you think

Suppose you sell $100,000 a year at a 10% net margin, so $10,000 profit. Raise prices 5% and lose no customers, and the extra $5,000 is almost pure profit: a 50% jump. Cutting costs by 5% rarely moves the needle that much. That's why pricing deserves deliberate thought, not a guess copied from a competitor.

Step 1: know your floor

Your floor is the lowest price that still makes money. Add up the variable cost of one unit or one job (materials, packaging, shipping, payment fees, your time at a fair rate), then add a share of fixed costs. The break-even calculator shows how volume and price interact. Never price below the floor except as a deliberate, time-limited promotion.

Step 2: understand customer value

Customers don't care about your costs; they care about what the purchase does for them. Ask: what does this save them in time or money? What does it earn them? What's the cost of the alternative? A bookkeeper who saves an owner ten hours a month at $60 an hour is delivering $600 of value, even if the work takes four hours.

Step 3: check the market

List five competitors and their prices for a comparable offer. You're not trying to match them; you're finding where you fit. If you're faster, more specialised, better reviewed or more convenient, price above the middle and say why.

Tiered pricing works especially well because many buyers choose the middle option, and the top tier makes the middle look reasonable. Use the markup and margin calculator to check each tier's margin.

Pricing services: hourly, project or retainer?

  • Hourly is simple but punishes you for getting faster and makes clients watch the clock. Calculate your minimum with the freelance rate calculator.
  • Project pricing rewards efficiency. Estimate hours, multiply by your rate and add 15% to 20% for revisions and scope creep.
  • Retainers give you predictable income. Define what's included each month and what counts as extra.

Worked example

A cleaner's costs per standard home visit are $18 in supplies and travel, plus three hours of labour valued at $25 an hour, so $93. Local competitors charge $110 to $160. Her reviews are excellent and she guarantees same-week booking, so she sets three tiers: Standard at $135, Deep Clean at $195 and Monthly Plan at $120 per visit with a four-visit commitment. Most clients choose the monthly plan, giving her predictable revenue at a healthy margin.

How to raise prices without losing customers

  1. Raise prices for new customers first and watch conversion.
  2. Give existing customers notice, usually 30 days, and a short reason.
  3. Pair the increase with a visible improvement where you can.
  4. Offer an annual prepay option at the old rate to reward loyalty.
  5. Expect a few customers to leave. If nobody objects, you were probably too cheap.

Frequently asked questions

Should I price low to get my first customers?

A launch discount with an end date is fine. A permanently low price trains customers to expect it and makes raising prices painful.

Do prices ending in .99 work?

Charm pricing can help in retail. For premium services, round numbers often signal quality better.

How often should I review prices?

At least once a year, and whenever your costs change significantly.
