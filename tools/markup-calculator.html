Markup vs margin: the mistake that costs owners money

Markup and margin both describe the gap between cost and price, but they measure it against different bases. Markup is profit divided by cost. Margin is profit divided by price. Because price is always bigger than cost, margin is always the smaller percentage. Owners who aim for a "50% margin" but add 50% to cost end up with a 33% margin and wonder where the money went.

Formulas

  • Price from markup = Cost × (1 + Markup %)
  • Margin from markup = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup)
  • Price from target margin = Cost ÷ (1 − Margin %)
  • Markup from margin = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin)

Worked example

A retailer buys a backpack for $40. With a 60% markup the price is $64 and profit is $24, which is a 37.5% margin. If the retailer needs a 45% gross margin to cover rent and wages, the price must be $40 ÷ 0.55 = $72.73, which is an 81.8% markup.

Price from margin, not markup, when you plan. Your operating costs are paid out of revenue, so margin tells you directly whether there is enough left over.

Frequently asked questions

What should my unit cost include?

Everything it takes to get one unit to a customer: purchase price, freight in, packaging, and per-order payment or marketplace fees.

Is keystone pricing still used?

Keystone, or doubling the cost (100% markup, 50% margin), is still common in retail as a starting point. Adjust from there for competition and perceived value.

Can margin ever reach 100%?

No. A 100% margin would mean the item cost nothing. The calculator shows a dash if you enter 100% or more.
