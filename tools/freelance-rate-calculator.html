Freelance rate calculator

Work backwards from the income you want to the hourly rate you need to charge, after taxes, expenses and the hours you can't bill.

Why most freelancers undercharge

New freelancers often divide their old salary by 2,080 working hours and charge that. It falls short for three reasons: you now pay your own taxes and benefits, you carry business expenses an employer used to cover, and you can't bill every hour you work. Sales calls, admin, invoicing and learning eat a big share of the week.

How the calculation works

  1. Gross up your take-home goal for tax: goal ÷ (1 − tax rate).
  2. Add a year of business expenses.
  3. Add a buffer for slow months and profit.
  4. Divide by billable hours: billable hours per week × working weeks per year.

Worked example

A designer wants $80,000 take-home, spends $9,000 a year on software, equipment and insurance, expects a 28% tax rate, bills 25 hours a week for 46 weeks and adds a 15% buffer. She needs roughly $138,000 in annual revenue across 1,150 billable hours, which is about $120 an hour or $600 for a 5-hour billable day.

From hourly rate to project price

Clients prefer a fixed price. Estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, then add 15% to 20% for revisions, meetings and scope creep. As you get faster, keep the project price: the efficiency is your reward, not a discount for the client.

Frequently asked questions

How many billable hours are realistic?

Many full-time freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week. Starting out, assume fewer while you build a client base.

Should I charge different clients different rates?

Yes. Rush work, larger companies and specialised skills justify higher rates. Keep a published floor so you never go below this calculated minimum.

When should I raise my rate?

When you're booked out more than a month ahead, or when most new prospects accept without negotiating.
